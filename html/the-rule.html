The Rule

Columbus, Ohio, 2026

Fiction. Owen Tully and everyone around him are invented. The book he reads, Paul Kingsnorth’s Against the Machine, is real; its ideas appear here as Owen understands them, in his words, not the author’s. Drafted by a language model at the site owner’s request, which is the kind of sentence Owen would have a great deal to say about.

February

Owen Tully read Against the Machine on a flight from Columbus to Denver in February, which is to say he read it on a screen, in a seat he had chosen from a diagram, at an altitude arranged by a system, eating almonds that a supply chain had brought from a valley he would never see, and by the time the plane began its descent he had highlighted forty-one passages and was in the grip of the particular fever that comes over a certain kind of man at twenty-nine when a book tells him that the shape of his unhappiness has a name.

The name was the Machine. Not the phone, not the algorithm, not any one company; Kingsnorth was insistent on that, and Owen, who worked in supply-chain analytics for a company that moved other companies’ goods and whose own job was, as far as he could tell, to make a number on a dashboard go down, felt the insistence land. The Machine was the whole thing: the totalizing system of technology, economy, and appetite that ate every limit it met — on speed, on scale, on what a person could be asked to become — and called the eating progress. It had no center and no off switch. Its promise was freedom from every constraint and its product was people who could not sit still in a room. The book did not think you could vote it out or regulate it or build a better version of it. It thought you could stop giving it your allegiance. It thought that was mostly a spiritual matter, and it said so, and Owen, who had not been inside a church since his grandmother’s funeral, found that this was the part he could not stop thinking about.

He was, I should say, exactly the reader the book would have been most cautious about. He had a podcast habit and a history of enthusiasms. In 2022 it had been stoicism; in 2024, a cold-plunge tub that was now a planter. He was intelligent in the way that makes a person good at adopting frameworks and bad at noticing he has adopted one. He was kind, restless, slightly vain about his reading, and lonely in a way he had no vocabulary for, living alone in a one-bedroom in a building with a package room and a gym, in a city he had moved to for the job, four hours from the town in the Hocking Hills where his parents still lived and where he had spent his adolescence wanting to leave.

March

He did the things. You could have predicted every one of them.

He deleted three social accounts and announced the deletion on the fourth. He bought a flip phone and kept the smartphone “for maps.” He ordered, from the largest retailer on earth, a cast-iron pan, a hand-crank coffee grinder, two books on permaculture, and a set of raised beds that arrived flat in a box the size of a door. He began saying “the Machine” in conversation, at first ironically and then not, and watched his friends’ faces do the thing faces do when someone has found a lens. He wrote, over a weekend, a long document titled Exit, which laid out a five-year plan for leaving the city, learning a trade, buying land, and building a life “at human scale,” and which he composed, without noticing the irony until Sunday night, with the assistant, which had also suggested the title.

The book had warned him about this. It was one of the things he had highlighted at thirty thousand feet: that the Machine was very good at selling you your own escape from it, that withdrawal could be one more product, that the man who moves to the woods to post about the woods has not moved anywhere. He had highlighted it and nodded and then done it anyway, because reading a warning and being warned are different things, and the second one only happens to you.

It happened in April. He had driven down to his parents’ place for Easter — he had gone to the service, for the first time in eleven years, and sat through it feeling like a tourist and also like something else he could not identify — and afterward, in the kitchen, he had explained the Machine to his father, at some length. His father, Dale, had been a lineman for the electric co-op for thirty years and had opinions about systems that ate their limits, none of which he had ever needed a book for. He listened. He was a good listener; it was one of the things Owen had left home to get away from. When Owen finished, Dale said, “So what are you going to do?” and Owen described Exit, the five years, the land, the trade.

“Which trade?”

Owen did not have one in mind. He had a category.

“Mm,” Dale said, and went out to see about the gutters, and Owen sat in the kitchen with the strong sense of having been told something, and it was on the drive back that night, on 33 north with the flip phone in the cupholder and the smartphone in his lap giving him directions to a place he had driven to a hundred times, that he understood what it was. He had read a book about limits and made a plan with none. He had read a book about place and made a plan to leave one. He had read a book that said the problem was in the soul and had responded by buying equipment.

May

The smaller version began, as smaller versions do, with a failure of the larger one. The raised beds went in on the apartment’s shared roof, with the building manager’s reluctant permission, and in May a woman named Bev from the sixth floor came up while he was watering and asked what he was growing and he told her, and she said her late husband had grown tomatoes on a fire escape in Cleveland for forty years and that Owen was doing it wrong, and showed him. She was seventy-three. She had lived in the building since before it had a package room. He had never spoken to her. He had lived four floors below her for three years and had not known she existed, and he stood on the roof with wet hands while she pinched suckers off his plants and told him about Cleveland, and he thought: this is it. Not the land. Not the trade. This.

What the book had said — and he went back and reread it that night, on paper this time; he had bought a copy at the used bookstore on High Street, which was a small thing but he counted it — was not that you should escape. It was that the Machine unmade the human by dissolving every limit that made a human life a particular thing in a particular place among particular people, and that the counter was not a better system but a life with edges. A place you were from and stayed. People you were bound to and could not optimize away. Work that produced something you could hold. Limits you chose and kept. And under all of it, a sense — he was still shy of the word — that the world was not raw material, that it was owed something, that there was a ground you did not make and could not buy. The book called that ground sacred and thought the culture that had forgotten it would forget everything else in order. Owen was not sure he believed that. He was sure he had felt something in the church at Easter that his frameworks did not have a slot for, and he had decided, on the roof, to stop pretending the slot was empty.

So he tore up Exit, which is to say he deleted the file and then, feeling that this was insufficient, printed it and burned it in the cast-iron pan on the roof, which Bev observed from her window and never mentioned. And he wrote, by hand, on one page, a rule.

June

I have the page. He gave it to me in November, when I told him I was writing this, and said I could print it if I did not make it sound better than it was. It is not a manifesto. It reads like a man trying to build a fence around a yard he has only just noticed he owns.

Stay. Do not plan to leave Columbus. Learn the building, the block, the parish. Give it ten years before deciding it is the wrong place.

Phone in the drawer at seven. Nothing with a screen after dark except a lamp. Sunday no screen at all.

Buy nothing from the big one. If I cannot get it from a person or a store I can walk into, ask whether I need it. Usually no.

Make one thing a week with my hands that someone else can use. It can be bad.

Go to church. Do not decide yet whether I believe it. Go the way you go to a language class: you don’t understand it, so you go.

Eat with people. Not at a screen. Twice a week minimum, one of them someone I did not choose.

Do not talk about the Machine. Do the thing instead. If they ask, say.

Do not post any of this.

He kept it about two-thirds of the time, which he considered, correctly, a good rate for a rule. The last line he kept entirely, and it was, he told me, the hardest and the one that did the most work, because it turned out that a very large part of what he had thought of as his life had been the anticipation of describing it, and when that was removed there was, for a while, an alarming silence in which he had to find out whether he wanted to do things that no one would know he had done.

August

The job was the hard part, and the book had not solved it for him, because the book was honest that it could not: you cannot live outside the Machine, only against it, from inside, and everyone has to eat. Owen’s job was making a number go down. He had believed, in March, that he would quit it. By August he had understood that quitting was the Machine’s own gesture — the clean break, the upgrade, the next thing — and that the harder discipline was to stay and put a limit on it. He stopped working past six. He stopped checking the dashboard on weekends, which produced two weeks of panic and then nothing, because the number went down or did not regardless of whether he was watching. He told his manager, in a conversation he had rehearsed with no one, that he would not take the promotion that came with the on-call rotation, and his manager, who was forty-four and had the face of a man who had not seen his children awake on a weekday in some time, looked at him for a long moment and said, “Okay,” in a tone Owen has been trying to interpret since.

What he did with the hours was small and I am going to list it, because the book’s whole argument is that the small things are not preparation for the real thing; they are the real thing, and the Machine wins precisely by convincing you otherwise.

He learned, from a man at the parish named Teodor who had done it in Romania, to keep bees, and there were two hives on the roof by August, which the building manager did not know about and Bev did. He fixed Bev’s kitchen faucet, badly, and then a neighbor’s, less badly, and by the end of summer was the person on three floors that people knocked for, which cost him roughly four hours a week and returned something he had not had in Columbus in three years, which was a reason to be in the building on a Saturday. He ate on Tuesdays at the parish supper, where the food was bad and the company was not chosen and he sat, for a month, next to a man named Curtis who had been sleeping in his car and who did not want to talk about it and did want to talk about the Browns, and they talked about the Browns, and in September Curtis had a room, not because of Owen, but Owen had been at the table, and the book would have said that was the point and Owen had begun, cautiously, to agree.

He went to church. He did not know, and does not know, what he believed. He went the way the rule said, like a language class, and by August he could follow the service without the book and had noticed that the hour a week in a room with no screens, in which nothing was being sold and no one wanted anything from him except that he stand and sit at the right times, had become the hour around which the rest of the week arranged itself. He would not call it faith. He told me it was more like discovering that a room in his house had been there the whole time with the door painted over, and that he had not yet gone all the way in, and did not intend to be rushed.

November

Here is where it stands, which is nowhere dramatic.

He still works for the company. The number still goes down. He has a flip phone and a smartphone in a drawer that comes out for maps and for calling his mother, who prefers video, and he has decided that his mother’s preference outranks his rule, which the rule, being his, permits. He has thirty pounds of honey he does not know what to do with and a list of neighbors who do. He has been to his parents’ place six times since Easter, which is more than the previous three years together, and on the last visit his father took him up a pole — a training pole, decommissioned, in the co-op’s yard — and showed him how a lineman climbs, with the gaffs and the belt, and Owen got eight feet up and came down shaking and Dale said, “Well, now you know what it’s like,” and that was the trade, as far as Owen is concerned; not one he will practice but one he has now been shown by the man who practiced it, which is a different kind of inheritance and the one he had actually wanted.

I asked him what the better future was, since that was the phrase he had used in March, in the document he burned. He thought about it for a while. He has gotten better at that; it is one of the visible changes.

“I thought it was a place,” he said. “Land, a trade, a different town. Something you get to. The book kind of lets you think that, if you read it fast, because it’s so angry at where we are. But that’s the Machine’s idea of a future — the next thing, the upgrade, the exit. The book’s actual idea, I think, is that the future isn’t somewhere you go. It’s what survives. It’s made of whatever people manage to keep while the thing eats everything else. A limit you hold. A table people sit at. A building where someone knows your name. A room with the door painted over that you go into anyway. The Machine doesn’t have a future; it just has more. The future is whatever’s still here when more runs out, and it’ll be here because somebody stayed and kept it. That’s the whole job. Stay and keep it.”

Then he said, because he is still Owen: “That sounded like a podcast. Don’t use it.”

I am using it. He will not see this; he does not read the site. He is, as of this writing, on the roof, winterizing two hives with a seventy-three-year-old woman who is telling him he is doing it wrong.

A note on what this story is doing. Kingsnorth’s book is a diagnosis, and its prescription is deliberately not a program: refuse the Machine’s terms, live with limits, stay somewhere, attend to what is sacred, keep what can be kept. That is easy to read as a call to escape, and Owen reads it that way first, because most of us would. The story is about the difference between adopting a book’s vocabulary and being changed by it: the second one only shows up as a rule you keep two-thirds of the time, in the place you already are, among people you did not pick, doing small things no one will know about. It sits with the other stories here — The Tuner, The Night Shift, The Draft — as one more answer to the same question, from a man who found the question in a book and the answer on a roof.
