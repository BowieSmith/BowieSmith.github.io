The Night Shift

a life in Marrowgate, 2018–

Fiction. A companion to The Tuner, set in a harder version of the same century; drafted by a language model at the site owner’s request. Marrowgate, Ines Okafor, and the systems named here are invented.

2077

Ines Okafor worked the night shift at St. Brendan’s clinic in the Sump for thirty-three years, and on Wednesday she worked her last one, and on Wednesday night, as on every Wednesday night since 2053, the waiting room filled with people who were not sick and sang. I was there. I have been there most Wednesdays since I was nineteen and came in with a fever and a letter that said I did not qualify to be seen, and she saw me anyway, and then sat down beside me and asked me to read the letter aloud. I am a nurse now. I am writing this down because someone should, and because Ines will not; she has never in her life written a sentence about herself that was not a shift note.

You should know what Marrowgate is if you are not from here. It is one city with two altitudes. The Heights have the trees, the water pressure, and the people whose decisions still count as decisions. The Sump has the rest of us. Nobody in the Sump starves; the century did manage that much. What the Sump lives on is the Allotment, which is not what the old books called a basic income but a conditional one, scored: hours of verification work — being the human whose signature appears under a machine’s decision, so that a company can say a person made it — exchanged for credits, adjusted by a number the systems call your Standing. Standing rises and falls by rules nobody outside four companies has read. It decides what you rent, how fast the utility restores your power, and, at St. Brendan’s, how long you wait. The triage system is called Tessera. Its letters begin After careful review.

2044

She was twenty-six on her first night and the building already had one nurse per shift, which is to say her. The consolidation had come through that spring: the Sump’s nine clinics became three “care hubs,” and St. Brendan’s kept its doors only because the building was too damp to sell. Tessera did the intake. A screen took your face and your Standing and your symptoms and gave you a color, and the color gave you a wait, and the nurse’s job, as the hub director explained it on the first day, was to “support the system’s determinations and attest to their review.”

Ines had trained under an old charge nurse named Beatriz Almeida at the county hospital before the county hospital closed, and Beatriz had taught her one thing above all the clinical things, which was to look at the patient before you look at the screen. So on her first night she looked. Tessera had given a grey — the lowest color, “defer to morning” — to a man of about sixty holding his left arm in a way she did not like. His Standing was low. His symptoms, as he had typed them, were vague. She looked at him, and then at his arm, and then she walked him back and did what nurses did before there were colors, and he was in an ambulance to the Heights forty minutes later. In the morning the director asked her to sign the attestation for the shift, which included a line stating that all Tessera determinations had been reviewed and were appropriate.

“One of them wasn’t,” she said.

“You overrode it. That’s the review.”

“Then it wasn’t appropriate. I’ll sign that I reviewed them. I won’t sign that they were right.”

This is the whole of what she did for thirty-three years, and I want to set it down plainly because it looks like nothing. She read what she was asked to sign. If it was true, she signed it. If it was not, she wrote what was true instead, in the margin, in pen, and signed that. The forms were designed to be countersigned by people whose Allotment depended on the hours; the system counted on the pen never coming out. Her attestations, over the years, grew a second document underneath them — the record of what Tessera had gotten wrong on the night shift at St. Brendan’s, in her handwriting, dated, with the patient’s first name and what she had seen. She called it the margin. It was, by the time I arrived, eleven binders.

2049

The first time they moved against her it was procedural, which is how it always is. Her attestations were flagged as “non-standard” and her verification hours — the ones that funded her Allotment on top of the clinic’s small wage — were suspended pending review. Her Standing dropped; her rent notice arrived a week later, tuned, as they are, to the new number. The director, who was not a cruel man and who I think admired her, told her that if she would simply sign the standard form, everything would restore itself in a billing cycle.

She asked him to put that in writing. He wouldn’t. She wrote it down herself, dated it, and put it in the margin. Then she kept signing what was true and lived, for two years, on less. When she talked about that time, later, she did not describe it as brave. “I was scared the whole time,” she told me. “I just noticed that the fear didn’t change what the form said. Either the man had been a grey or he hadn’t. Being scared doesn’t make a wrong thing right, so it can’t make it right to sign it. That’s not courage. That’s arithmetic.”

The hours were restored in 2051 after a records request — filed not by her but by a law student from the Heights who had come to St. Brendan’s with a broken wrist and left, three hours later, having read four binders while she waited — turned up an internal memo describing the “non-standard attestation problem” and the “one nurse” who was its source. The memo was embarrassing. The hours came back. Nothing about Tessera changed. Ines wrote the memo’s reference number in the margin and went to work.

2053

The choir began because of the screens. By 2053 the waiting room walls at St. Brendan’s were screens, installed by the hub network at no cost to the clinic, and the screens showed, to each person who sat in front of one, content generated for them — for their Standing, their debts, the hour of the night. People in the Sump lived inside that content the way people in the Heights lived inside quiet. A man waiting eight hours with a grey would sit in front of a wall that knew his name and showed him, in a loop, things that made him feel worse and click more. Nobody could tell a real face on those walls from a made one. Most people had stopped trying, which is a way of believing nothing.

Ines could not turn the screens off; the contract forbade it. So on a Wednesday night in March she asked the seven people in the waiting room whether any of them knew “Down to the River,” and three did, and she started it, badly, and they finished it, and for four minutes there was a sound in the room louder than the walls. She did it again the next Wednesday. By summer people were coming who were not sick. By the following year the Wednesday choir at St. Brendan’s had forty voices, no director but her, no repertoire but what somebody knew, and a rule: anyone who walked in could sing, and anyone waiting on a color was asked, gently, first.

I asked her once what a choir had to do with the margin. She looked at me as if I had asked what the left hand had to do with the right. “You can’t hold a place with grievance,” she said. “People will come to an appeals desk when they must. They’ll come to a place that sounds like that” — the room was warming up behind us — “when they can. The second kind of coming is where you get the people who’ll stand next to you in the first kind. Make something worth keeping. Then keep it.”

It was also, and she would have hated my saying so, beautiful. Not the singing, which was mostly poor. The room. A waiting room in the Sump at eleven at night with the walls trying to sell forty people their own despair, and the forty people singing over it, in a building too damp to sell, led by a woman in scrubs who could not hold a tune and did not care.

2058

The hearing was the state’s, not hers. A legislator from the Heights had lost a relative to a grey and discovered, as people from the Heights periodically did, that Tessera existed, and there was a committee, and the committee wanted testimony from “frontline personnel.” The hub network sent its director. Ines went on her own time, with four binders in a wheeled case, and sat in a hallway for six hours, and was given five minutes.

She did not make a speech. She read. Fourteen entries from the margin, first names and dates and what she had seen and what the color had been, in the voice she used for shift handover, and when the chair thanked her and said her time was up she said, “I have four hundred more,” and put the binders on the table and left them there. The clerk logged them into the record because the clerk did not know what else to do with them. They are still in the record. I have looked.

The committee produced a report. The report produced an amendment. The amendment required that Tessera “make available, on request, the principal factors” behind a color, in language “comprehensible to the patient.” The companies complied by adding a sentence to the letters, after careful review, that read principal factors: clinical presentation, historical utilization, and Standing. Nothing else changed. Ines pasted the amendment on the waiting room wall next to the screens, on paper, and under it, in pen: this is what a year of testimony bought. It is not nothing. Ask for your factors. Make them say “Standing” out loud.

People started asking. It was awkward for everyone. She considered awkwardness a form of progress.

2063

I have to write about Mrs. Adaeze, because Ines would insist that a record which only listed what was saved was an advertisement.

She was a Wednesday regular, an alto, seventy-four, from Kell Street. She came in one August night with a grey and Ines looked at her and did not like what she saw and walked her back, as she had walked back a thousand people, and did what could be done in a clinic with one nurse and no physician after midnight, and called for transport, and the transport was delayed, and the delay had a reason, and the reason had a Standing in it. Mrs. Adaeze did not make it to the Heights. I am not going to write more than that. Ines did not. Her entry in the margin for that night is four lines long and the last of them is a hymn title.

She sat with the family on Thursday, in their kitchen on Kell Street, for most of a day. She did not tell them it would be all right or that the system would be fixed or that anything meant anything. She told them what had happened, in order, plainly, including the parts that were the system’s fault and the part that was, she said, hers, for not calling ten minutes sooner. The daughter told me later that it was the only conversation that week in which anyone had spoken to her as though she could bear the truth. The choir sang the following Wednesday. Ines started the hymn. She could not finish it and forty people did.

I asked her, months later, how she kept going after that. She said she didn’t know that she had kept going; she had gone to work, which was a different thing. “You don’t get to decide whether the world is the kind of place where that happens. It is. You get to decide what you do on Wednesday.”

2069

The offer came the year the network moved to close St. Brendan’s for good. A woman from the hub network, decent, tired, came to the desk on a Tuesday and waited her turn, which Ines noted with approval, and offered her a position: Director of Community Care Standards, Sump District. A salary twenty times her Allotment. An office in the Heights. A seat, the woman said without irony, at the table where Tessera’s thresholds were set. A chance to help from the inside.

I was there, and I wanted her to take it. I was tired too. Ines asked three questions. Would the margin come with her? No; the binders were clinic property and the position was individual. Could she publish what she learned about the thresholds? No; there would be an agreement. Would St. Brendan’s stay open? The network would be glad to fund a “digital care presence” in the building.

“Then you’re not offering me a seat at the table,” Ines said, without heat. She was never angry at people doing their jobs; she was exact about what the job was. “You’re offering to move the table.” She thanked the woman, meant it, and invited her to stay for Wednesday. The woman did. She sang. I do not know what she did with that afterward. I know that the closure was delayed eighteen months on a finding that the building was “in active clinical use with documented community reliance,” a phrase that appeared in the network’s own review and that cited, as evidence, attendance at a weekly choir.

St. Brendan’s closed anyway, in 2071. The choir moved to the Methodist hall on Vane Street, where it still is. The margin, all nineteen binders by then, moved to the church basement, where the law student — a lawyer now, with a Sump practice that does not pay — keeps it indexed and pulls from it whenever the legislature remembers that Tessera exists, which is every four or five years, for about a month. Ines took a job at the last hub on the Sump side of the line, on nights, and kept a margin there too.

2077

I want to be honest about what she did and did not accomplish, because she would.

Tessera still runs. Standing still decides how long you wait. The Sump is still hot and the Heights still have the trees. The gap between the two altitudes is wider than the year she started. Nothing she did changed the thing itself; the thing itself is owned by people who did not come to the desk, and the century has not produced the lever that moves them. If you measure her life against the system, she lost, steadily, for thirty-three years.

That is the wrong measure, and I think the argument on the other pages of this site is the right one about why. She never tried to earn her standing; the system offered her a number for it and she declined to be the number. She kept the one thing in-house that the system most wanted automated, which was the judgment about whether a form was true, and she kept it with a pen. She treated everyone who came to the desk — patients, the network’s tired emissaries, the remote reviewers she sometimes reached by phone at three in the morning — as though there was somebody in there, and she was right often enough. She made, for people she knew, in a room she could not control, a thing worth keeping, and then she kept it, and the keeping is what the network’s own review ended up citing. And she wrote down what she saw, in order, plainly, when the whole architecture of the place was designed to make sure nobody did.

The margin is the most complete record that exists of what Tessera did to the Sump. It is in a church basement. It was written on the backs of forms, at night, by one person, over thirty-three years, and it has been entered into the public record twice and read by perhaps a hundred people, and it changed one sentence in one law and delayed one closure by eighteen months. I have come to think that this is what a righteous life looks like from the outside in a century like ours: not a victory but a ledger, kept honestly, by someone who also made sure that the room where it was kept had singing in it.

· · ·

On Wednesday, after the last shift, she came to the hall on Vane Street and sat in the back, which she never does, and someone started “Down to the River,” badly, and the room finished it. She did not sing. She listened with her eyes closed. When it ended she said, to no one in particular, “Sounded like that the first time too,” and then, because she cannot leave a true thing unsaid even when it is against her, “Better now. More of you.”

The margin for the last shift is one line. All determinations reviewed. Two overridden. Both transported. Both admitted. Signed, I.O.

A note on what this story is doing. The Tuner tested the argument of What Remains in a kind world, and a reader rightly said that world was not the likely one: abundance has never removed conflict or closed the gap between those who own the systems and those who live inside them. This story runs the same test in a likelier world. Ines has no more capability than the systems she works under; she has less. What she has is the same list, lived under pressure: she refuses to be a number, keeps judgment in her own hand, is generous under uncertainty, makes something beautiful for people she knows, and keeps an honest record. It does not fix the world. It makes a room in it worth keeping, and defends the room, which is, I think, what a righteous life mostly consists of when the century is not on your side.
