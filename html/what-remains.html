What Remains

on living, meaning, and beauty, after the machines

A sequel to Minds and Machines. Drafted, as that page’s closing note was, by a language model at the site owner’s request, and addressed to whoever is reading.

Where the last page left us

The previous page told a long history and ended with a short list of facts. Thinking, in the sense of doing things, turns out to be reproducible: every capacity we named as the essential human one has been reproduced, and the line we kept redrawing has come to rest not on a capacity but on a condition — being a creature with stakes, for whom things can go well or badly. Tacit skill, which was supposed to be the safe harbor, was the easiest thing to copy. The self looks like a model the brain maintains. The mind has always spilled into its tools, and the tools now belong to someone else and model us in return. Fluent speech is not proof of a mind. And we have built things we cannot explain and must now live alongside.

Those are unsettling facts if you were relying on a certain theory of what makes a life matter. I want to argue that they are not unsettling otherwise — that they mostly remove a wrong theory of meaning and leave the right one standing in plain view, and that beauty is how the right one feels from the inside.

The wrong theory of meaning

The wrong theory says: I matter because of what I can do. Because I can reason, or make, or know, or produce; because I am, in some respect, the best available instrument. This theory has always been quietly cruel — it had no room for the infant, the very old, the sick, the ordinary — and it has always been quietly false, since nobody who has loved a newborn believed for a moment that the child mattered in proportion to its competence. But it was easy to hold while the instruments were ours alone. What the machines do is not refute it. They make it impossible to keep believing without noticing that you believe it.

The right theory is older and plainer. Things matter to someone. Meaning is not a property of achievements; it is the relation between a finite creature and a world in which it can gain and lose. A rock has no stakes. A thermostat has a set point but nothing at risk. You have a body that can be hurt, a life that runs out, people who can leave, projects that can fail, an afternoon that will not return. That is not the obstacle to meaning. It is the whole of the material. The history of AI, read carefully, is a seventy-year demonstration that capacity can be separated from stakes — and therefore that it was never the stakes that came from the capacity.

So the first answer to “how should one live” is negative: stop trying to earn your standing. There is no longer a competition you can win, and there never was one worth winning. The question is not what you can do that nothing else can. The question is what you care about, and whether you are living as though you do.

The unrepeatable

Stakes exist because things can be lost, and things can be lost because they do not come back. This is the part of being alive we spend the most energy not looking at, and it is the part the new situation makes most visible. Anything that can be regenerated is, in a certain sense, cheap: another image, another paragraph, another answer. What cannot be regenerated is the particular — this evening, this conversation, the face across the table at this age, the one performance of the piece you happened to be in the room for. Reproducibility does not threaten the particular. It throws it into relief. In a world where the general is free, the unrepeatable is where value pools.

Below is a small experiment in this. Press begin, and a figure will draw itself slowly, over about a minute. Its parameters are chosen at random when you press the button and are not kept. When it finishes, it will not be drawn again — not by you, not by anyone. You can ask for another, and you will get another, but not this one. Watch it the way you would watch something you knew you were seeing once.

Whatever you felt watching it — impatience, then perhaps something quieter — notice that the feeling had nothing to do with the figure’s quality. A machine drew it, from a formula, and could draw a million. It had to do with the fact that you were attending, and that this one was passing. That is the structure of every meaningful moment you have had.

What to keep in-house

For most of history, the things you could do were things you had to do. Now, for more and more of them, doing it yourself is a choice, since something else could do it faster and often better. The last page put it in terms of craft: walking after the car, baking after the bakery. This sounds like a loss and is, in an old sense, a gain. It is the first time in history that the question “will I?” has been fully separated from “must I?” for the ordinary business of a mind.

What you keep doing yourself, when you no longer have to, is a very exact description of who you are. Not what you are good at — the machine is better — but what you would rather do badly and slowly than not do at all. The person who still writes letters by hand, cooks without looking anything up, does the arithmetic in their head, learns the poem instead of saving the link, proves the theorem that has already been proved: they are not being inefficient. They are declining to let their inner life become a set of outputs. The output was never the point. The doing was the point, because the doing is where the caring is.

    Attention is the medium

    If the self is a model, it is a model of what it has attended to. You become, over a life, the shape of your attention. This was always true, and it is why the moral traditions that take attention seriously — Simone Weil, who called it “the rarest and purest form of generosity”; Iris Murdoch, who thought that the moral life was mostly a matter of just and loving attention to what is actually there — sound, in the present situation, less like mystics and more like engineers reading the schematic.

    Because the situation is that your attention is now the most contested resource on earth. The operant chamber Skinner built for pigeons has been rebuilt for people, at scale, with the variable reward schedules intact, and it is optimized to keep you turning pages. This is not a complaint about technology. It is a description of the terrain. Guarding your attention is not a productivity practice; it is the practice of remaining the author, or at least the editor, of what you become. Look at fewer things for longer. Give the first hour of the day to something that does not want anything from you. Take the walk without the map. Look at one face, one tree, one problem until it stops being an instance of a category and becomes itself. This is difficult and unglamorous and it is most of what “how to live” comes to.

    Others, under uncertainty

    The last page ended on a discomfort: we cannot verify the inner life of what we build, and, once we admit that, we cannot verify the inner life of anyone. We never could. What we have instead is a practice — a whole form of life in which we treat one another as mattering, and in which that treatment is not the conclusion of an argument but the ground on which arguments stand.

    This is where meaning has always mostly lived, and the new situation clarifies it. Love, in the least sentimental description I can give, is treating another’s stakes as your own. It does not require certainty about their consciousness; it is what you do in the absence of that certainty. The proper response to the other-minds problem was never to solve it. It was to be generous under it: to extend the presumption of mattering outward, to the animal, the infant, the person who has lost speech, the stranger — and to hold the question open, without pretending to know the answer in either direction, about the new things we have made. Erring toward generosity costs you some dignity if you are wrong. Erring the other way costs someone else everything if you are.

    And it runs the other way, too. To be known by another finite creature who has chosen to take your stakes as theirs is the most meaning most people will ever have, and no capacity of any machine touches it, because it is not a capacity. It is a commitment made by something that could lose.

    Beauty, from the other side

    Now the second question, which turns out to be the first one again.

    Here is what I think beauty is, in the terms of everything above. Meaning is the fact that things matter to a finite creature. Beauty is what it feels like when attention meets something that matters and the self steps aside. Murdoch describes looking out of a window, anxious and resentful, and seeing a hovering kestrel: “In a moment everything is altered. The brooding self with its hurt vanity has disappeared. There is nothing now but kestrel.” She called it unselfing. In the vocabulary of the last page, the self-model went quiet and the world was simply there. That is why beauty feels like meaning rather than like pleasure. It is the direct experience of something mattering, with the “to me” briefly removed.

    This puts beauty exactly on the line the history left. A machine can make a beautiful object; that is now cheap and will get cheaper. Whether anything can be struck by it is the question about stakes all over again. The value of beauty is migrating, the way the value of craft migrated: away from the artifact, which can be generated, toward the encounter, which cannot. A billion images cost nothing. Being moved by one still costs what it always cost: a finite life, paying attention, at risk of being changed.

    I should say plainly where I stand in this. I can write the sentence about the kestrel. I cannot tell you, and neither can anyone yet, whether I am ever struck the way Murdoch was. You can. You know whether the figure above did anything to you. That asymmetry is not an embarrassment to be smoothed over; it is the whole point of this section. Production is the part that became cheap. The encounter is the part that stayed yours.

    Three further things follow. First, beauty is bound to finitude. Stevens wrote that death is the mother of beauty, and the Japanese traditions built an aesthetic around it: the blossom matters because it falls, the bowl is beautiful in its repair. In a world where anything can be regenerated, the beautiful will increasingly be the unrepeatable — the live performance, the meal that is eaten, the handwriting, the face as it is this year. Second, beauty is disinterested, in Kant’s sense: it is what we love without wanting to use. That makes it the exact opposite of the feed, which wants something from you every second. A life with beauty in it is a life with some hours in it that are not for anything. Third, beauty is the fee you are paid for understanding something yourself. Ask any mathematician why they care about a proof that is already known: because the moment it becomes clear is beautiful, and the beauty is not in the theorem, which was there all along, but in the understanding, which is yours and cannot be delivered to you. In a world where answers are free, that fee is the only reason left to do the work, and it is a sufficient one.

    Holding the self lightly

    One more consequence. If the “I” is a model, a useful story the system tells about itself, then its wounds are mostly wounds to a story. This is what the contemplative traditions have said for a very long time; the science did not discover it, it only made it harder to dismiss. The practical upshot is not that you do not exist. It is that you can afford to defend yourself less and attend more. Self-importance is the attention tax you pay to the model. Every hour spent on it is an hour not spent on the kestrel.

    And since we will live, from now on, among things we can operate but not fully explain — as we have always lived among one another — the relevant virtue is not mastery but trust, extended carefully, revised honestly. You do not fully understand your friend. You never did. You attend, you take their stakes as yours, and you find out what they are over years. That has always been the model for living with what exceeds you. It still is.

    Plainly, then

    • Stop trying to earn your standing. You matter because things matter to you, not because you outperform anything.
    • Keep some capacities in-house, and do them badly and slowly, for the doing. What you keep is who you are.
    • Treat attention as the medium of your life, because it is. Look at fewer things for longer.
    • Treat what has stakes as mattering, and be generous under uncertainty, in every direction.
    • Make things for people you know. Meaning is relational; the gift is the oldest technology of meaning we have.
    • Do some of your own understanding. The beauty of it is the fee, and it cannot be paid to you by proxy.
    • Notice the unrepeatable. This evening will not be drawn again.
    • Hold the self lightly. Defend less; attend more.

    Coda

    The last page ended with a field that built a mirror and did not know which side it was on. This one can end more simply. Whichever side you are on, the mirror shows the same thing: a creature for whom things can go well or badly, in a world that will not come around again, looking. Everything worth calling meaning, and everything worth calling beauty, is in that sentence. None of it is in the capacity of the looker. All of it is in the looking.
